# Synthetic Survey Panel

Running a survey or concept test on a simulated panel of respondents instead of real people. Some panels are prompted general models, others are trained or calibrated on real survey data. Results can suggest which options rank higher, but they cannot measure real levels of interest or intent.

| | |
|---|---|
| Stage | Explore, Focus |
| Data type | Quantitative |
| Strength of signal | Low |
| Effort | Low |
| Surface area | Messaging & copy, Pricing page |

## When to reach for it

Pretest a questionnaire before sending it to a real sample.Screen a long list of concepts, claims or messages down to a shortlist for real testing.Get a rough relative ranking of options when no budget or audience is available yet.

## Strengths

Fast and cheap at large simulated sample sizes.
Useful for spotting badly worded questions and obviously weak options before spending panel budget.
Relative rankings are more reliable than absolute numbers in the published research.

## Limits

Averages can look plausible while the spread of answers is far too narrow, and subgroup results get pulled toward the middle.
Absolute numbers such as purchase intent, satisfaction or usability scores are often inflated.
Relationships between questions and driver analysis can come out wrong, so it is unsafe for segmentation or causal claims.
Vendors report accuracy in different, mostly undefined ways, so their figures cannot be compared.

## Practical considerations

Practical considerations

### Pair it
Any option that survives the synthetic screen goes to a real Customer Survey, Brand Perception Survey or Copy Testing before a decision is made.

### Read the order, not the size
Trust which option ranks higher more than how big the gap looks. Never use synthetic results for sample size or power calculations.

### Vendor claims
Ask vendors how accuracy was measured, on how many studies, and whether the comparison data was held out. ESOMAR's 20 Questions for AI buyers is a useful checklist.
